Amazon has blocked Meta's personal AI agent, Muse, from making purchases on Amazon.com on customers' behalf. It's the newest front in the fight over who controls the shopping experience — and the customer relationship — when AI agents transact for people.

## In brief

- **What happened:** Amazon cut off Muse from buying on Amazon.com. Users now see a message: "Continued access by an unauthorized AI agent violates Amazon's Conditions of Use, to which our customers have agreed."
- **What Muse is:** Meta's free personal AI agent, launched September 8, capable of multi-step tasks across email, calendar, payments, dining, and shopping (iOS, Android, muse.ai, WhatsApp). It became the #1 free app in the U.S. App Store within a week, ahead of ChatGPT. Meta says the agent runs in "a secure virtual machine" with its own browser and requires user approval for sensitive actions like purchases.
- **Conflicting claims:** Amazon says permission was never obtained, the agent doesn't identify itself, and it appears to capture and store customer credentials (a privacy/security risk). Its spokesperson: third-party apps that buy on behalf of others "should operate openly and respect service provider decisions." Meta didn't respond to this incident; it has previously said Muse "has no visibility into people's passwords or payment methods," with credentials going "into secure storage, so Muse can use them without seeing them." The two accounts conflict.
- **Precedent and context:** Amazon previously sued Perplexity; in August a court ruled that it's the *user*, not the AI company, that accesses Amazon's computers — so Amazon lost its injunction and is now using a contract/Conditions-of-Use approach. It has also moved to block Google's and OpenAI's shopping agents. (The two companies have a chip deal and a FB/IG-Amazon shopping partnership dating to 2023 — so competition and cooperation are intertwined.)

## Our take

This is the agentic-shopping fight turning real. The honest core: two giants disagree on the facts — Amazon says Muse harvests credentials and hides itself, Meta says it never sees passwords or payment. We can't referee that from outside, so hold both. The bigger point for our audience: the "an AI agent shops for you across the web" future isn't guaranteed; platforms will decide, via terms of service and lawsuits, whether outside agents can transact on their turf. For merchants, that means agentic commerce may arrive walled-garden by walled-garden — indeed, Amazon's own "Buy for Me" feature identifies itself and lets brands opt out, a hint that platforms prefer agents they control over third-party ones. We saw a version of that model last week in [Anthropic's Claude for Commerce move](/en/news/anthropic-claude-for-commerce), built on platform partnerships. Nothing to do today, but if you sell online, watch how your platforms treat outside agents — it will shape who owns the customer relationship.
